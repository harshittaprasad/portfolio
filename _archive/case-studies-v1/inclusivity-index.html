01 — Overview

Inclusive design that looks past the usual two diagnoses.

Most research on neuroinclusive UX centres on autism and ADHD, usually in education. This paper looks at the adults it tends to leave out, people with OCD, Tourette’s, sensory processing differences and the anxiety that often comes with them, and at the everyday, high-stakes places they meet friction: healthcare portals, work software and online checkouts.

It argues for an evidence-based approach led by empathy rather than a deficit model, and treats neurodiversity as a source of better design for everyone.

02 — The gap

Usable isn’t the same as comfortable.

  • A narrow focus. Guidelines built around attention and executive function miss needs like endless re-checking with OCD, or interfaces that punish accidental clicks with Tourette’s.
  • Compliance isn’t comfort. WCAG sets the baseline, but says nothing about whether an experience feels safe and calm to use. The question has to move from “is this usable?” to “is this comfortable and welcoming?”
  • Nothing measures sensory stress. Usability testing barely touches sensory or emotional comfort, so there’s no standard way to show that sensory-friendly design helps.

03 — Method

Neurodivergent adults as co-designers, not test subjects.

A mixed-methods study with formal IRB approval and emotional safety built in: decompression breaks were always there, and anyone could pause or leave an activity at any time, to avoid cognitive or sensory overload.

25

adults, 18 and over

5

neurotypes: autism, ADHD, OCD, Tourette’s and sensory processing disorder

3

research phases, from listening to co-designing

Three phases

Phase 1

In-depth interviews

Moments where digital experiences break down, especially in high-stakes settings: sensory triggers, and how the “just right” feeling of something being complete shows up online.

Phase 2

Card sorting

Real interface pieces from e-commerce and healthcare sites, grouped by what felt urgent, actionable or less draining. Participants’ mental models didn’t match how developers usually organise them.

Phase 3

Co-design and testing

Together, participants redesigned a classic pain point, a clunky multi-step application form, and the prototype was tested against the original.

Measuring what people feel

Cognitive comfort

Modified NASA-TLX

Mental demand, effort and, above all, frustration and anxiety after each task.

Sensory load

Sensory Overload Scale

A custom five-point self-report of distress from specific sights, sounds or movement, before and after each task.

Emotional safety

A one-question anxiety scale

A quick visual scale to catch shifts in how people felt right before and after difficult moments.

04 — Findings

Finishing a task isn’t the same as being fine.

The paper expects people to complete their tasks on both versions of the form, but the original to leave them far more drained, with markedly higher NASA-TLX and Sensory Overload scores than the co-designed prototype (p < .01). The friction is real even when nothing visibly fails: it’s an invisible mental toll.

What the interviews and tests pointed to

OCD and Tourette’s

Uncertainty is the frustration

Clear, always-visible status and quick ways to undo, so no one gets stuck double-checking everything.

Sensory processing

Motion spikes stress

Spinning carousels and flashing alerts drive distress up. Interfaces need a way to turn off all non-essential motion, not just a mute button.

ADHD and autism

Actions first

People wanted to see what they could do, Pay, Edit, View, rather than dig for it in categories like “Preferences”.

05 — The framework

E‑DUX, the Empathy-Driven UX framework.

Everything the study heard comes together in three principles for calmer, more flexible digital products.

01

Predictable pacing and control

People set the pace: no timed sessions on important forms, auto-save that just works, undo and exit buttons you can’t miss, and status that says where you are, like “Step 3 of 5”.

For OCD, Tourette’s, executive function and anxiety

02

Sensory sanctuary

No surprise sounds or flashing lights. Let people turn off unnecessary animation, switch to gentle colours and adjust density, like bigger text or more space between lines.

For sensory processing, autism and Tourette’s

03

Adaptive information density

Information in small, logical groups of three to five, collapsible sections so nothing overwhelms, and action labels tested with real people rather than guessed.

For ADHD, autism and executive function

06 — For everyone

Designing for the edges improves the middle.

The paper argues these aren’t special add-ons. Turning off parallax and flashing alerts lowers sensory load for everyone, including people with vestibular issues, anyone working under stress, and people on mobile. Chunking information to what working memory can hold, about four items at a time, makes a dashboard easier for anyone to skim in a hurry.

What starts as a neurodivergent need becomes a new standard for clarity.

07 — What comes next

From early evidence to everyday practice.

  • Scale and time. Twenty-five participants give rich detail and early proof, not the whole picture. Next is E‑DUX in a large company or healthcare system, tracking fatigue and frustration over months rather than one session.
  • A standard measure. Validating the Sensory Overload Scale against established emotional and sensory tools, so it’s ready for industry-wide use.
  • Across cultures. Testing how ideas about urgency, predictability and information structure translate across languages and cultures.
